== Range Safety: Security by Design

> Or, Breaking It Before They Do

Every rocket launch has a Range Safety Officer, and they hold the one button nobody wants pressed: the flight termination command. If a rocket strays off course towards a town, they end the flight.

Here's the kicker: that power is exactly what lets the launch director say "go" with confidence. If things go wrong, someone already has the authority and the plan to stop it.

Security in the Gnostic Enterprise should work the same way. It isn't the department of "no". It's the discipline that lets your agile squads ship quickly, lets your super employees use AI freely, and lets your board sleep at night.

=== The Flood

Every publicly disclosed security flaw gets an identifier, known as a CVE (Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures). For years the number published each year crept up steadily, from around 18,000 in 2017 to just under 50,000 in 2025. Then it took off. By 23 October 2026, more than 88,000 had been published, and the year was not over{empty}footnote:[NIST National Vulnerability Database, CVEs counted by publish date via the NVD CVE API 2.0, to 23 October 2026, https://nvd.nist.gov/vuln/search]. In the thirty days to 23 October alone, an average of roughly 566 new CVEs were published every single day.

No person can read every one of those. No team can either.

Yet only a small fraction are known to be used by attackers. In 2025, the US Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA) added 245 entries to its catalogue of vulnerabilities known to be exploited in the wild, the KEV list{empty}footnote:[CISA Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue, entries added in 2025, https://www.cisa.gov/known-exploited-vulnerabilities-catalog]. Severity alone is a poor guide: a critical flaw nobody is exploiting may matter less than a moderate one on the KEV list. The skill is no longer reading everything. It's knowing which handful affect _your_ code, and fixing those first.

Meanwhile, attackers now use AI to find and exploit holes around the clock. AI takes no weekends off, doesn't wait for a change window, and couldn't care less that your penetration test is booked for next spring.

=== Why the Annual Pen Test Won't Save You

Confession time: for many organisations, security still runs on a calendar. A penetration test once a year, patching once a quarter, and a PDF report that a few people read before it's filed.

A pen test is a snapshot of how secure you were on the day. By the time the report lands, thousands more advisories are out, your squads have shipped dozens of changes, and a flaw being exploited today might wait months for the next patch cycle. The attacker only needs one gap.

We learnt this lesson with delivery, swapping one big annual release for small, frequent, measured changes. Security has to make the same move – from an event to a loop.

> A pen test that ends with a PDF has done half the job. The value is in the merged fix.

=== AI on Defence

The good news? The same technology works for the defenders. Frontier AI models can read code, reason about whether a flaw is really exploitable in your particular system, and write the fix.

The pattern is simple. Automation checks every new advisory against the exact software you run and bins the ones that can't touch you. AI reasons about what's left. A human decides whether to accept the fix. It's the super employee applied to security: each of your security people gets the reach of a much larger team.

=== The Loop: Attack, Defend, Fix, Report

The security world has long talked about a *red team* that attacks and a *blue team* that defends. When the two work as one, it's a *purple team*. Continuous security is a purple team loop:

. *Attack*: test your own systems the way a real adversary would, continuously and with permission.
. *Defend*: check whether your monitoring saw the attack. Every miss becomes a new detection.
. *Fix*: close the hole with a change and a test proving it's closed, then attack again to confirm.
. *Report*: track every finding, and measure the trend.

Remember Schrödinger's cat from the chapter on the power of data? What isn't measured might as well not exist – and with security, that holds right up until the day it very much does. So give security its own mission control: a single score, plus a few monthly measures such as exploitable issues open now, time to detect an attack, and time from advisory to merged fix.

=== A Worked Example: The Purple Team

At KnowAll, we've built this loop as a crew of AI agents, called the Purple Team{empty}footnote:[KnowAll AI Purple Team, https://www.knowall.ai/purple-team]. It's not the only way to do it, but it makes the loop concrete.

- *Pennie the Pen Tester* is the red team. With written authorisation, she probes apps, APIs and cloud like a real adversary, mapped to the OWASP Top 10, the OWASP LLM Top 10 and MITRE ATT&CK, and checks each new CVE against the code to prove what is really exploitable.
- *Ollie the Operator* is the blue team. He filters the daily CVE flood using CISA KEV and EPSS (the likelihood a flaw is exploited next), watches Microsoft Defender for Cloud and Sentinel around the clock, and turns every missed attack into a new detection. Fixes arrive as pull requests with a proving test, for your own developers to review and merge.
- *Poppie the Planner* runs the loop. She scopes the work, tracks every finding as a risk, issue or task, and reports a security score, time to detect and time to fix each month.

The agents work inside the tools a team already runs, and because security data is sensitive, the agents and models can run entirely inside your own tenant.

The point isn't the product. It's the shape: continuous testing, continuous monitoring, fixes delivered as reviewable changes, and a score leadership can see.

=== Guardrails: Security by Design

Letting AI agents attack your systems and change your code sounds alarming – and it should. The guardrails matter more than the tools. A Range Safety Officer doesn't rely on good intentions; they rely on rules agreed before the countdown starts.

The flight rules from the previous chapter apply in full: least privilege, a named identity for every agent, audit trails, a kill switch, and a human approving every change that matters. Security adds a few of its own, whether the work is done by people or AI:

- *Written authorisation*: targets, timings and rules of engagement agreed in writing first, with anything risky run against a staging copy.
- *Zero trust and encryption*: nothing is trusted just because it's "inside" the network, and data is encrypted in transit and at rest by default.
- *Identity first*: role-based access control (RBAC) and multi-factor authentication (MFA) for everyone, and everything, that touches your systems.

None of these are new. What's new is that they now apply to AI agents too. Security by design means building them in from day one, not bolting them on after an incident.

=== New Risks for a New Kind of Colleague

If your super employees work alongside AI agents, those agents become part of your attack surface. The OWASP Top 10 for Large Language Model Applications catalogues the main risks; three are worth every leader knowing:

- *Prompt injection*: an attacker hides instructions in something the AI reads, such as an email or a web page, and the AI follows them. Social engineering for machines.
- *Data leakage*: sensitive information ends up in prompts, outputs or places it shouldn't, often unnoticed.
- *Agents with too much access*: an agent given broad permissions "to be helpful" can do broad damage if misled. OWASP calls this excessive agency.

So treat an AI agent like a new member of staff: the flight rules give it a name, limited access and an off switch, and you supervise it in proportion to the risk.

=== What Leaders Should Ask

You don't need to be a security specialist. You need the right questions:

- How many actively exploited vulnerabilities affect us right now, and how long have they been open?
- If we were attacked today, how long would it take us to notice?
- How long from a published advisory to a merged fix?
- Is our security score improving month by month, and who owns it?
- Which people, and which AI agents, can change production, and can we switch them off?
- What data can our AI tools see, and where does it go?

Then act. Put security on your mission control alongside delivery and happiness, fund it as a continuous capability rather than an annual event, make it part of every squad's definition of done, and, as the previous chapter argued, give the Centre of Excellence ownership of the guardrails.

Back to the launchpad. That termination button is the reason the rocket is allowed to fly at all. Give your organisation the same: eyes on every threat, a fast way to close it, and the authority to stop anything – or any agent – that strays. Then launch as often as you like.

> Security is not the reason we cannot launch. It is the reason we can.
